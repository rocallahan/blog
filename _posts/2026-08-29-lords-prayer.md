It's amazing how the [Lord's Prayer](https://www.churchofengland.org/faith-life/what-we-believe/lords-prayer), so short, so simple and so old, just keeps on giving. Just tonight I was half-dozing, praying it and thinking about it and seeing from new angles.

_Our Father in heaven,_\
_hallowed be your name,_\
_your kingdom come,_\
_your will be done,_\
_on earth as in heaven._

I have to confess I often fall into the pattern of thinking that God is aloof --- that he watches and judges and answers some prayers, but fundamentally leaves us to ourselves; the world is *our* kingdom. But these lines convict me; our vision is for this to be God's kingdom, not in some indefinable future, but increasingly now. And what is God's kingdom? That's a deep theological question in itself, but the prayer  gives us a good answer: where God's will is done. Where God's righteousness, love, justice and mercy prevail.

_Give us today our daily bread._\
_Forgive us our sins,_\
_as we forgive those who sin against us._

A perennial tension in Christian thought: should those of us who follow Christ expect to no longer sin? Of course we don't want to sin, but if we do sin, does that mean our profession of faith is illegitimate and Jesus will say to us, "I never knew you"?

Often people answer this in the negative quoting [1 John 1:8-9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%201&version=NIV), but actually I think the Lord's Prayer here is even clearer: Jesus expects us to continue to need forgiveness for our ongoing sins. The context suggests so strongly this is not one-time forgiveness for old pre-conversion sins: "give us today our daily bread" is clearly ongoing prayer for an ongoing need, and of course forgiving those who sin against *us* is too.

_Lead us not into temptation,_\
_but deliver us from evil._

"Deliver us from evil" on its own reads naturally as a plea to deliver us from evil inflicted on us from without, and surely it includes that. But it's so easy to self-righteously assume that's the whole focus --- that we saints beg for protection from unrighteous "others". Jesus powerfully undermines such thinking by reminding us we are prone to sinning: we ask God to deliver us not only from the sins of others, but our own sinful nature as well.
